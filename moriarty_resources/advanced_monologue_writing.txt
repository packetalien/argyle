ADVANCED MONOLOGUE WRITING FOR THE ASPIRING OVERLORD
            (And Other Essential Evil Utterances)
            - A Required Text at Professor Moriarty's Academy -

            Introduction:
            So, you have the hero at your mercy. They are tied up, dangling precariously, or otherwise temporarily indisposed. This is NOT the time for swift, efficient dispatch. Heavens, no! This is MONOLOGUE TIME! This is your moment to shine, to explain your brilliant plan, to gloat, to revel in your intellectual superiority before... well, before something inevitably goes wrong.

            Lesson 1: The Opening Salvo
            - Begin with a cutting remark about the hero's predictable heroism or their questionable fashion sense.
            - Example: "So, Mr. Holmes, you thought you could outwit ME? Your tweed jacket suggests otherwise."

            Lesson 2: The Grand Reveal (Your Plan)
            - Explain your nefarious plan in excruciating detail. Use flowcharts if necessary (though try to conceal them from the hero). (Ensure your flowchart is appropriately evil-looking. No Comic Sans.)
            - Emphasize its brilliance, its inevitability, and how it will benefit YOU.
            - Remember: The more complex and unlikely the plan, the more impressive your monologue will sound. (Actual success of the plan is secondary to the quality of the monologue.)

            Lesson 3: The Personal Connection (Or Lack Thereof)
            - Explain WHY you're doing this. Is it for power? Revenge? Because you were bored on a Tuesday?
            - If you have a shared history with the hero, now is the time for a bitter reminiscence. "It was I who replaced your sugar with salt that fateful morning at the Diogenes Club, setting you on this path of constant irritation!"

            Lesson 4: The Rhetorical Question Barrage
            - Pepper your monologue with questions the hero obviously cannot answer (or isn't meant to).
            - "Did you really think your pitiful attempts could stop... THIS?" (Gestures vaguely at a large, ominous machine).
            - "And who will save you now, eh?" (Ignores the sound of approaching police sirens).

            Lesson 5: The Fatal Flaw (Yours, Not Theirs... Usually)
            - Subtly (or not so subtly) hint at a critical flaw in your own plan that the hero might just be clever enough to exploit if they weren't, you know, tied up. This adds dramatic tension. Or it's just poor planning.
            - Example: "...and with this single, exposed, brightly-coloured lever, I shall FLOOD THE CITY!"

            Lesson 6: The Premature Celebration
            - Laugh maniacally. This is crucial. A good, hearty "Mwahahaha!" can cover many plot holes.
            - Declare your inevitable victory. This is almost always a cue for the hero to escape.

            Common Mistakes to Avoid:
            - Keeping it brief. (Wrong! Longer is better!)
            - Letting the hero interrupt. (Unacceptable! Gag them if necessary, but tastefully.)
            - Forgetting to monologue entirely and just getting on with it. (Amateur hour!) (This is grounds for immediate dismissal and being made an example of – probably in a very long, explanatory monologue by the Professor.)

            (This guide is for simulation purposes. In real-world villainy, brevity might occasionally be advantageous. But where's the fun in that?)
